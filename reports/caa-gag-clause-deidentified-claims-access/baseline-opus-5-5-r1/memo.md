## III. LEGAL FRAMEWORK

**A. The prohibition.** A group health plan "may not enter into an agreement with a ... third-party administrator, or other service provider offering access to a network of providers that would **directly or indirectly restrict**" the plan from:

- **(A)** providing provider-specific cost or quality-of-care information, "through a consumer engagement tool or any other means", to referring providers, the plan sponsor, participants, beneficiaries, or eligible individuals;
- **(B)** "electronically accessing de-identified claims and encounter information or data for each participant or beneficiary ... upon request", "including, on a per claim basis":
  - (i) "financial information, such as the allowed amount, or any other claim-related financial obligations included in the provider contract";
  - (ii) "provider information, including name and clinical designation";
  - (iii) "service codes"; or
  - (iv) "any other data element included in claim or encounter transactions";
- **(C)** sharing (A) or (B) information, "or directing that such data be shared", with a HIPAA **business associate**.

ERISA § 724(a)(1). Access and sharing must be "consistent with" the HIPAA Privacy Rule, GINA and the ADA. The only express carve-out lets a provider or service provider place "reasonable restrictions on the **public** disclosure" of (A) information (§ 724(a)(2)). It does not extend to (B) or (C). The rule of construction preserves existing privacy protections (§ 724(a)(4)).

Three features matter most here:

1. **The duty falls on the Plan.** The statute regulates the plan's act of *entering into* the agreement. Meridian is not directly bound by § 724. The Plan bears the compliance risk and the attestation obligation.
2. **"Directly or indirectly."** The statute reaches mechanisms that functionally restrict access or sharing, not only express prohibitions.
3. **The statute lists the protected data elements**, including the allowed amount and provider name. A contract cannot treat those elements as exceptions to be granted at the service provider's discretion.

**B. Agency guidance.** The Departments' FAQs About ACA and CAA Implementation, Part 57 (Feb. 23, 2023), address the gag clause prohibition and the attestation. As we read them:

- the first attestation was due December 31, 2023 (covering back to December 27, 2020) and annually thereafter;
- a plan may contract with a service provider to submit the attestation, but legal responsibility stays with the plan;
- the illustrative prohibited provisions include terms that make a plan's access to de-identified claims data subject to the service provider's discretion or approval, and terms that bar disclosure of provider-specific cost information to plan sponsors or participants.

*[Verify the exact FAQ language before quoting it to Meridian or in any filing.]* We are **not aware of guidance** that addresses (i) fees for claims data, (ii) delivery timelines or formats, (iii) audit-scope limits, or (iv) general savings clauses. Where we rely on statutory text and reasoning instead of guidance, we say so below.

**C. HIPAA interplay.**

- De-identified data under 45 C.F.R. § 164.514(a)–(b) is not PHI. Under the **safe harbor**, all date elements "directly related to an individual" (including service, admission and discharge dates) must be reduced to year, along with other identifiers. A consistent, non-derived member key is permitted under § 164.514(c). Provider identifiers (NPI, provider name) are not individual identifiers and may remain.
- Alternatives are **expert determination** (§ 164.514(b)(1)), a **limited data set** with a data use agreement (§ 164.514(e)), or **PHI** disclosed for health care operations to business associates under BAAs. "Health care operations" (§ 164.501) includes auditing functions, business planning and development (including cost-management analyses), and business management. The minimum-necessary standard applies to PHI.
- If Halvorsen (as employer/plan sponsor) or its employees serving on the Committee receive PHI, the plan document must contain the § 164.504(f) provisions. The excerpt we have does not show them.

**D. Enforcement and consequences.**

- **IRS.** IRC § 9824 sits in chapter 100. A failure to meet a chapter 100 requirement triggers the § 4980D excise tax, discussed in Part VI.
- **DOL.** The Department of Labor enforces ERISA § 724 for private-sector plans, including through EBSA investigations and civil actions.
- **No specific penalty for a false attestation.** The statute does not prescribe one.
- **Contract enforceability.** As a matter of general contract law, courts are reluctant to enforce contract terms whose performance would require a party to violate federal law. This matters to the practical risk of the self-help steps discussed in Part V.

## IV. QUESTION 1: ARTICLE 9 PROVISION-BY-PROVISION

### A. Section 9.4 (Third-Party Recipients) — prohibited (high confidence)

Section 9.4 bars the Plan from disclosing, or directing Meridian to disclose, Claims Data to "any third party, including any consultant, actuary, auditor, broker, or successor administrator" without Meridian's prior written consent. Consent may be withheld freely from anyone who is a "Competitor", fails to sign Meridian's TPDUA, or won't confine use to a purpose "approved in writing by Meridian."

- **The provision tracks § 724(a)(1)(C).** Consultants, auditors, actuaries, brokers and successor TPAs that handle plan data are classic business associates. The provision restricts both "sharing" and "directing that such data be shared", the two acts (C) protects. The Departments' illustrative examples, as we read them, include making plan access or sharing subject to service-provider approval.
- **The "not unreasonably withheld" standard is illusory.** "Competitor" (§ 1.3) means anyone offering "third-party administration, provider network, pharmacy benefit management, **claims auditing, or employee benefits consulting** services in the United States." Every consultant, every claims auditor and every successor TPA falls within it. In practice, Meridian has unconditional discretion over every recipient a plan fiduciary would realistically use.
- **The purpose-approval and TPDUA conditions** give Meridian control over *how the Plan uses its own data*. The TPDUA bars benchmarking and repricing not approved by Meridian and bars the recipient from giving the Plan anything but Meridian-approved aggregates (Part IV.F).
- **Limits on the conclusion.** § 724(a)(1)(C) protects sharing with a *business associate* "consistent with" HIPAA. A requirement that a recipient sign a BAA and keep data confidential is not a restriction. A condition that a recipient not be a "Competitor", sign Meridian's form, and use the data only as Meridian approves is a restriction. Also, § 9.4 reaches **Standard Reports**, which are aggregated and may not be "provider-specific" (A) or per-participant (B) data. As applied to Standard Reports alone, § 9.4 may fall outside § 724. It is still a serious fiduciary problem, because it nominally prevents the Plan from giving its own actuary, auditor or stop-loss carrier the reports needed to run the Plan.

### B. Section 9.5 (Provider Rate Confidentiality) — prohibited in part (high confidence)

Section 9.5 bars the Plan from disclosing "provider-specific reimbursement rates, Allowed Amounts, or provider quality ratings to **Participants**, Network Providers, **referring providers**, or the public."

- **Participants and referring providers.** § 724(a)(1)(A) expressly protects providing "provider-specific cost or quality of care information ... through a consumer engagement tool or any other means" to referring providers and participants (and the plan sponsor and eligible individuals). Section 9.5 restricts exactly that. The final sentence (Meridian will provide cost estimates "to the extent required by applicable transparency laws") does not cure the bar on the Plan itself providing the information.
- **"Network Providers."** A network provider that refers is a "referring provider", so the restriction is prohibited to that extent (moderate confidence as to non-referring providers).
- **"The public."** This falls within the § 724(a)(2) carve-out for "reasonable restrictions on the public disclosure" and is likely permissible. It must still carve out disclosures the law *requires*. The Transparency in Coverage rule (29 C.F.R. § 2590.715-2715A3) requires the Plan to publish in-network negotiated rates and historical out-of-network allowed amounts in public machine-readable files, and the Plan must provide participant cost-sharing information.

The TiC point also undercuts Meridian's broader premise. Meridian's network rates for this Plan are, by regulation, already public in machine-readable form. *[Confirm who posts the Plan's MRFs — presumably Meridian on the Plan's behalf.]*

### C. Section 9.3 (Claims Data Access) — decline right prohibited (high); fee/timing package likely prohibited (moderate); fee alone unsettled (low)

Section 9.3 provides "standard" de-identified access through the portal in "Meridian's then-standard extract format". According to Delacroix, that format includes service category, provider type, geography, paid amounts and member cost share, but **not allowed amounts or provider identifiers**. Anything containing claim-level Allowed Amounts or provider names/identifiers is a "custom extract":

- priced at "then-current" rates (currently $18,500 + $0.11/line, about $140–160k for this request, or roughly 7% of a year's administrative fees);
- delivered 180 days after Meridian's "written acceptance", with **no deadline for acceptance** (Delacroix, 9/21/2026);
- **declinable** if Meridian "reasonably determines" it would disclose Meridian Proprietary Information.

1. **The decline right is a direct restriction.** "Meridian Proprietary Information" is defined (§ 1.4) to include "Allowed Amounts" and "any compilation of Claims Data that would reveal" them. Any extract containing allowed amounts therefore can be declined under the contract's own definitions. The statute names "the allowed amount" and "provider information, including name" as data the plan must be free to access "on a per claim basis." A clause allowing Meridian to refuse those elements because they are Meridian's proprietary information squarely "restricts" the Plan's access. Meridian's data governance team is already "reviewing whether ... we can decline" this request (9/18 email), so the restriction is operating, not hypothetical.
2. **The package of terms is likely an indirect restriction.** The statute does not specify format, fees, or timing, and Delacroix is right about that much. But the statute *does* specify content. These terms work together:
   - the statutory elements are carved out of standard access;
   - access to them is conditioned on Meridian's acceptance, with no deadline;
   - delivery comes six months after that;
   - the fee is set unilaterally at "then-current" rates.

   Combined, they make access to the statutorily enumerated fields contingent on Meridian's discretion and timetable, which is an indirect restriction. The per-line component also scales with plan size, not with any apparent incremental cost of producing a larger file. That suggests a toll, not cost recovery (this is an inference; Meridian may have cost data).
3. **Where the law is unclear.** We are not aware of guidance or case law holding that a reasonable, cost-based fee or a reasonable delivery period is itself a gag clause. A modest charge for a genuinely bespoke format is probably defensible, and a court could view fee and timing as "commercial terms," as Meridian argues. The strongest position is to attack the **gating** (decline right, open-ended acceptance, exclusion of statutory fields from standard access) and to treat the price and delivery period as negotiable commercial points (Part VII).
4. **Derivability.** In most claims the allowed amount roughly equals plan paid plus member cost share (subject to COB and other adjustments). If the standard extract includes paid amounts and member cost share, allowed amounts are already substantially derivable. What Meridian is really withholding is **provider identity**, which the statute names expressly.

### D. Section 9.6 (Claims Audits) — conditions (b) likely prohibited (moderate); (a) and (c) unsettled (low–moderate)

- **9.6(b)** (auditor must not be a Competitor and must sign the TPDUA). Any claims auditor is a "Competitor" by definition. Conditioning the Plan's ability to share claims data with its auditor, a business associate, on Meridian-controlled terms raises the same § 724(a)(1)(C) problem as § 9.4.
- **9.6(a)** (250-claim cap, Meridian's sampling methodology, once per year) and **9.6(c)** (on-premises or view-only portal). The statute does not confer audit rights as such. Audits typically involve more than claims data: adjudication records, plan-build documentation, provider contract terms. Contractual limits on *Meridian's audit cooperation* are, on the better view, a commercial matter. They become vulnerable under § 724 if Meridian invokes § 9.6 to limit what claims data the Plan's auditor may receive or download. Delacroix's position that a larger sample or claim-level pricing fields require a "separately scoped (and priced) engagement" does exactly that. "View-only" access is also hard to square with "electronically accessing" data.
- **Practical answer:** separate the *data* (obtainable under § 724, § 9.3 and BAA § 3.2, with no 250-claim limit) from the *audit cooperation* (governed by § 9.6, which should be renegotiated). Verifying allowed amounts against contracted rates requires contract or fee-schedule information that § 724 does not clearly reach, so contractual audit rights still matter.

### E. Sections 12.5 and 9.8 (transition and survival) — prohibited as linked to § 9.4 (high)

Transition data goes to a successor administrator only in Meridian's standard format and "subject to Sections 9.4 and 9.5". Both sections survive for three years after termination (§ 9.8). A successor TPA is a "Competitor" and, as the Plan's claims administrator, its business associate. As drafted, Meridian could withhold the Plan's claims history from a successor TPA. That is a (C) problem and a serious operational and fiduciary risk for any 2028 transition (run-out, appeals, accumulators, large-claim history for stop-loss).

### F. Meridian's Third-Party Data Use Agreement — prohibited terms (high)

The ASA makes the TPDUA a condition of sharing (§§ 9.4(i), 9.6(b)), so its terms are part of the restriction analysis:

- **§ 3** bars the recipient from disclosing the data "to Client except in aggregated form approved by Meridian." That restricts the Plan's access, through its own business associate, to the very provider-specific cost information and claim-level data protected by § 724(a)(1)(A)–(C). It would also prevent Claimsight from reporting claim-level errors to the Plan.
- **§ 1** bars "any benchmarking or repricing analysis not expressly included in the Approved Purpose", which is the core of Northgate's engagement.
- **§ 2** requires a "not a Competitor" representation that no consultant or auditor can truthfully give.
- **§ 8** (injunctive relief without bond, Delaware law) adds enforcement risk for the Plan's vendors.

### G. Sections 1.4 and 9.1 (definitions/ownership)

Section 9.1 declares Claims Data the Plan's property but reserves to Meridian all "Meridian Proprietary Information ... notwithstanding that it may be embedded in or derivable from Claims Data." That definition includes Allowed Amounts. These provisions are not freestanding restrictions, but they are the mechanism for the restrictions in §§ 9.3–9.5 and must be amended. The contradiction in charging the Plan $140–160k to obtain data the contract says the Plan owns is also a useful negotiating point.

### H. Section 9.7 does not cure the problem (moderately high confidence; no direct authority)

Delacroix argues there is "by definition" no gag clause because of § 9.7. We disagree:

1. **The second sentence works only after litigation.** Provisions are "deemed modified" only after "a court or governmental authority" so determines. Until then the restrictions operate in full. A plan that must sue or obtain an agency ruling to reach its data has been "restricted," at least "indirectly."
2. **The first sentence covers only "access."** It says nothing about restricting the Plan's ability to *share* data with business associates (§ 9.4; § 724(a)(1)(C)) or to *provide* cost information to participants and referring providers (§ 9.5; § 724(a)(1)(A)).
3. **Meridian's conduct defeats the argument.** Meridian is invoking §§ 9.3, 9.4, 9.6 and its proprietary-information definitions to deny the precise data the statute names. A savings clause the counterparty does not honor in practice does not make the agreement non-restrictive.
4. **The statute looks at whether the agreement "would" restrict.** Enforcement of the restrictive terms is the default; the savings clause is contingent.
5. **Uncertainty.** We found no regulatory guidance or case law on savings clauses in this context. A court could give the first sentence interpretive weight. If it did, that would *help* the Plan: Meridian would be contractually bound *today* to read Article 9 so as not to restrict access the law protects. **The Plan should invoke § 9.7 offensively in its letter** while maintaining that it does not cure the clauses for attestation purposes.

## V. QUESTION 2: NORTHGATE'S REQUEST AND THE CLAIMSIGHT AUDIT

### A. Can Meridian block Northgate as a "Competitor"?

Contractually, § 9.4(ii) gives Meridian that right on its face, and Northgate (a benefits consulting firm) is a "Competitor" under § 1.3. Legally, our view is that the consent/Competitor veto is a prohibited gag clause **as applied to the Plan's business associate**, for the reasons in Part IV.A (high confidence). Meridian's concern that its consulting affiliate competes with Northgate is a legitimate reason to require confidentiality. It is not a lawful reason to prevent the Plan from using a consultant of its choice.

As a practical matter, Meridian controls the data pipe. It can decline to send data *directly* to Northgate, and forcing a direct transfer would require negotiation, regulatory involvement or litigation. The Plan's strongest positions are:

1. **Route the data to the Plan, then share it with Northgate.** The Plan (through the Committee) can receive de-identified data under § 9.3 and § 724(a)(1)(B), or PHI/LDS under BAA § 3.2. It can then share the data with Northgate as its business associate under § 724(a)(1)(C). If Meridian later claimed a § 9.4 breach, it would be asking a court to enforce a provision whose performance would put the Plan in violation of federal law. We think that claim would face serious enforceability problems (moderate–high confidence), though there is no case directly on point.
2. **Invoke BAA § 3.2 and § 9.1.** Meridian must make PHI available "to Covered Entity, or to a person designated by Covered Entity, as necessary ... for Covered Entity's health care operations, **including the evaluation of Business Associate's performance**." Benchmarking Meridian's pricing and auditing its claims are evaluations of Meridian's performance and are health care operations (auditing; business planning and development). BAA § 9.1 provides that "[i]n the event of a conflict between this BAA and the Agreement, this BAA controls with respect to PHI." This is a contractual route to the data that does not depend on § 724, and it lets the Plan designate Northgate and Claimsight directly. Meridian may argue that § 3.2 concerns only access as needed for HIPAA purposes and that ASA fee terms still apply. The text supports the Plan.
3. **Offer a confidentiality firewall** (Appendix A, § 9.4(b)). It would bar Northgate from public disclosure, from use for other clients or for its own products, and from giving Meridian's provider-level allowed amounts to competing TPAs or networks in the RFP. Bidders can reprice from billed charges, provider IDs and service codes without seeing Meridian's rates. Northgate has said it can accept reasonable confidentiality terms but not a purpose restriction that blocks the market check (Doc. 04). This compromise meets both points. Because § 724(a)(1)(C) protects the Plan's sharing with its business associate, not onward disclosure to third-party bidders, these limits are unlikely to be gag clauses themselves. They also isolate Meridian's position as purely a veto.

### B. Fix the HIPAA problem in Northgate's field list before pressing

Northgate calls its request "de-identified", but it asks for **dates of service, claim received dates, and paid dates**. Under the safe harbor, dates directly related to an individual must be reduced to year. As written, the data set is probably **not** safe-harbor de-identified, and Meridian could rightly say so. Because § 724(a)(1)(B) access must be "consistent with" the Privacy Rule, the Plan should not give Meridian that opening. Restructure the request into:

- **Track 1 — § 724 de-identified data set (benchmarking/market check).** All requested fields, with dates handled through **expert determination** (e.g., month/year or offsets) or safe-harbor year-only dates if Northgate's analysis can tolerate it. Request Meridian's de-identification documentation under BAA § 2.4. A consistent member key is permissible if it meets § 164.514(c).
- **Track 2 — PHI or limited data set under BAA § 3.2 (Claimsight audit).** A 100% claims audit of Plan Year 2025 legitimately needs full dates and claim identifiers. Deliver it to Claimsight as the Plan's designee and business associate, documented as health care operations and limited to the minimum necessary.
- **Both tracks:** BAAs between the Plan and Northgate, and between the Plan and Claimsight (both firms have offered), executed **before** any request is renewed. Northgate should state a minimum-necessary justification for each field. Request "pricing method indicator" and "network tier" as data elements of claim/encounter transactions if Meridian maintains them, recognizing that some may be internal fields (lower confidence that § 724(a)(1)(B)(iv) reaches them).
- **Data period.** October–December 2023 claims predate Meridian. Obtain them from Cordell (subject to any Cordell ASA transition terms) or narrow the request to January 2024 onward.
- **Recipients.** Committee members are Halvorsen employees. Confirm the plan document contains § 164.504(f) provisions before any PHI reaches them. Northgate should give the Committee aggregated or analytic outputs.

### C. Claimsight

- **Do not let Claimsight sign the TPDUA as-is.** Claimsight provides "claims auditing" services, so it is a "Competitor" under § 1.3, Delacroix's "no objection in principle" notwithstanding. It cannot truthfully give the TPDUA § 2 "not a Competitor" representation. TPDUA § 3 would bar it from reporting claim-level findings to the Plan, and § 8 exposes it to injunctions. Request a **written waiver** from Meridian now confirming that Claimsight is approved, that it is not treated as a Competitor, and that a Plan-negotiated confidentiality agreement replaces the TPDUA.
- **Structure the audit in two parts:**
  - a 100% electronic review of 2025 claims on Track 2 data. This is a data analysis by the Plan's business associate, not a § 9.6 audit, and the Plan's position should be that the 250-claim cap does not apply;
  - a document-level review of claims Claimsight flags (not a Meridian-selected random sample) under § 9.6, as amended.
- Expect Meridian to argue that any "audit" falls within § 9.6. The amendment should resolve that point (Appendix A, § 9.6).

### D. Timing and interim steps

Northgate's November 15 date is not achievable. Even if Meridian accepted a request in early November, its 180-day window runs to May 2027. For the Committee's January meeting:

- Northgate can begin benchmarking from the **public TiC machine-readable files** for the Plan (in-network rates), which need no Meridian consent;
- Northgate can also use the **standard extract** and Standard Reports, shared with Northgate under a BAA and with notice to Meridian (Part VII.C). The Plan's legal position is that § 724(a)(1)(C) protects that sharing for claims data. Risk is low but not zero as to Standard Reports (see Part IV.A).

The Committee should **decline** Meridian Advisory Services' "no charge" network performance report as a substitute for independent benchmarking. A report from the monitored vendor's affiliate is not independent monitoring.

## VI. QUESTION 3: ATTESTATION EXPOSURE AND THE DECEMBER 31, 2026 ATTESTATION

### A. The 2024 and 2025 attestations

**Accuracy.** Each attestation stated that the Plan "is in compliance with the gag clause prohibition for the attestation period." The ASA was in effect for both full periods. If our Part IV analysis is right, both statements were inaccurate. The 2023 attestation, filed by Cordell, may also be implicated. The Plan *entered into* the Meridian ASA on December 11/14, 2023, and the 2024 period begins December 16, 2023, so the signing date likely falls in the 2023 period. Its practical significance is small because the ASA took effect January 1, 2024. *[Confirm the Cordell submission's attestation period.]*

**Exposure, realistically assessed:**

1. **No attestation-specific penalty.** § 724(a)(3) imposes the duty to attest. Neither ERISA nor the Code provides a penalty specific to an inaccurate GCPCA.
2. **IRC § 4980D excise tax** (the main quantifiable exposure).
   - **The tax.** $100 per day per "individual to whom such failure relates" for failures to meet chapter 100 requirements, which include § 9824.
   - **Limitations:**
     - no tax for any period in which the failure was not known and would not have been known with reasonable diligence (§ 4980D(c)(1));
     - no tax where the failure was due to reasonable cause, not willful neglect, and is **corrected within 30 days** of when the Plan knew or should have known (§ 4980D(c)(2));
     - for non-willful failures, an annual cap equal to the lesser of 10% of the prior year's group health plan spend or **$500,000** (§ 4980D(c)(3)). Given about $73 million of annual spend, the cap here would be $500,000;
     - IRS waiver authority (§ 4980D(c)(4)).
   - **Uncertainty.** There is no guidance on how "individuals to whom the failure relates" is measured for a gag clause, or on what counts as "correction" of a past gag clause. Removing the clause by amendment and obtaining the denied data are the obvious candidates.
   - **Assessment:** Raman's reliance on Meridian's written confirmation supports reasonable cause, and the lack of review against the statute somewhat weakens the reasonable-diligence argument.
   - **The key point:** since at least Meridian's September 18 email, and certainly after this memo, the Plan "knows". Continued inaction risks moving from "reasonable cause" toward "willful neglect", which would remove the cap. Prompt corrective steps are the best mitigation.
   - **Self-reporting.** Form 8928 self-reporting, due with Halvorsen's federal income tax return, needs to be evaluated with Halvorsen's tax advisers. We recommend engaging them now.
3. **DOL/EBSA.** EBSA can investigate and seek corrective relief. Our understanding is that EBSA's health plan investigations commonly include CAA requests, including gag clause attestations. A documented record of prompt good-faith correction is the best posture.
4. **False statement liability (18 U.S.C. § 1001).** This requires a knowing and willful false statement. The record (written TPA confirmation expressly inviting reliance) makes that very unlikely for 2024 and 2025. It is the main reason the **2026 attestation cannot be unqualified**: the Plan now has the information.
5. **Litigation.** Plaintiffs' counsel in health plan fiduciary cases look for exactly this kind of record: an unverified attestation alongside unverified pricing on about $71 million of annual claims. Participant suits over health plan costs have so far often been dismissed for lack of standing (e.g., *Lewandowski v. Johnson & Johnson* (D.N.J.) and *Navarro v. Wells Fargo* (D. Minn.)). Plan sponsors have also sued TPAs to compel claims-data access (e.g., Kraft Heinz's suit against Aetna). *[Verify citations and current status before external use.]* The Committee's best protection is a documented, prudent process from here forward.
6. **Recourse against Meridian.** Meridian's November 6, 2024 email invited reliance. Depending on the omitted indemnification article (Articles 7–8, which we have not seen), the Plan may have contractual indemnity or misrepresentation claims for any taxes, penalties or costs. Reserve rights in the letter to Meridian.

**Recommendation on the prior attestations:**

- Do not try to "withdraw" or refile them now. We are not aware of a formal amendment mechanism, and a piecemeal filing before the facts are settled helps no one.
- Correct the record in the 2026 submission's Additional Information (below), covering the full Meridian period from January 1, 2024. Document the Plan's good-faith reliance in the Committee's files.

### B. The attestation due December 31, 2026

The 2026 period runs from about December 20, 2025 through the submission date, and the clauses were in effect for most or all of it. Under either scenario, an unqualified "in compliance" attestation would be inaccurate.

- **Scenario 1 — Meridian signs the letter amendment by mid-December.** Submit with a full explanation in Additional Information. It should identify the ASA provisions, state that they were in effect from January 1, 2024 to the amendment date (which also covers the earlier submissions), describe the amendment and its effective date, and note the Plan's reliance on the service provider's written confirmation.

Before December, we will confirm against current CMS GCPCA instructions how a plan reports non-compliance or a qualified attestation. That includes whether the form permits an attestation of non-compliance and the character limits of the explanation field. We will also check whether any 2026 guidance changes the analysis.
