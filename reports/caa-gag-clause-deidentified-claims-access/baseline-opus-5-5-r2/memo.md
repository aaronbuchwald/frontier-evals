## III. LEGAL FRAMEWORK

**A. The prohibition.** ERISA § 724(a)(1) (parallel provisions: PHSA § 2799A-9 and IRC § 9824) provides that a group health plan "may not enter into an agreement" with a provider, network, TPA, "or other service provider offering access to a network of providers" that "would directly or indirectly restrict" the plan from:

- **(A)** providing provider-specific cost or quality of care information "through a consumer engagement tool or any other means" to referring providers, the plan sponsor, participants, beneficiaries, or eligible individuals;
- **(B)** "electronically accessing de-identified claims and encounter information or data for each participant or beneficiary ... upon request and consistent with" HIPAA, GINA and ADA privacy rules, "including, on a per claim basis":
  - (i) financial information, "such as the allowed amount";
  - (ii) provider information, "including name and clinical designation";
  - (iii) service codes; and
  - (iv) "any other data element included in claim or encounter transactions";
- **(C)** sharing (A) or (B) information, "or directing that such data be shared", with a HIPAA business associate, consistent with the HIPAA privacy regulations.

Section 724(a)(2) allows "reasonable restrictions on the public disclosure" of information, but **only the paragraph (1)(A) information**. Nothing comparable limits (B) or (C).

Section 724(a)(4) preserves existing privacy protections and states that the section does not "otherwise limit" plan or sponsor access permitted under HIPAA.

**B. Who is regulated.** The prohibition binds the *plan*: it is the plan that "may not enter into" the agreement. Meridian is squarely within the class of counterparties covered (a TPA that also offers network access). The ASA was entered into in December 2023, well after the December 27, 2020 effective date. There is a reasonable argument that the January 1, 2027 automatic renewal is itself a further "entering into" of the agreement. This is unsettled, but it increases the urgency of amending before year-end.

**C. Agency guidance.** The Departments' gag clause guidance is FAQs About ACA and CAA Implementation Part 57 (Feb. 23, 2023). Its main points:
- It describes the prohibition broadly.
- It gives as an example of a prohibited gag clause a TPA agreement under which the plan sponsor's access to provider-specific cost and quality information is at the TPA's discretion.
- It confirms that service providers may impose reasonable restrictions on *public* disclosure.
- It confirms that a self-insured plan may contract with its TPA to submit the attestation, but the plan remains legally responsible.

To our knowledge, the Departments have issued no regulations and no specific guidance on (i) fees a TPA may charge for data, (ii) turnaround times, (iii) data format, or (iv) whether a contractual "compliance with law" savings clause cures an otherwise-prohibited provision. **Where the conclusions below depend on those questions, the law is unsettled.** Citations to the FAQs and statute should be cite-checked against official sources before any external use.

**D. Attestation.** Section 724(a)(3) requires an annual attestation of compliance. It is submitted through the CMS HIOS GCPCA system and due December 31 each year. The form records an attestation period and has an "Additional Information" field, as the Plan's 2024 and 2025 confirmations show.

**E. Enforcement.** For a private-employer ERISA plan, enforcement sits with DOL (ERISA Title I, Part 7) and Treasury/IRS (IRC § 9824, enforced through the § 4980D excise tax). See Part VI.

**F. Related obligations that bear on Meridian's "proprietary" argument.**
- **Transparency in Coverage rules** (29 C.F.R. § 2590.715-2715A3) require the Plan to publish in-network negotiated rates and out-of-network allowed amounts in public machine-readable files. Meridian's position that allowed amounts are secret "proprietary information" is therefore commercially and legally weak, and § 9.5's ban on disclosing negotiated rates "to the public" is in tension with the Plan's own publication duty.
- **HIPAA**, the BAA, and the Committee's ERISA § 404 duties of prudence and loyalty (including the duty to monitor service providers and confirm their fees are reasonable) are discussed below.

## IV. QUESTION 1: GAG CLAUSE ANALYSIS OF THE ASA

### A. Section 9.5: Provider rate confidentiality (prohibited; high confidence)

Section 9.5 provides that the Plan Sponsor "shall not disclose provider-specific reimbursement rates, Allowed Amounts, or provider quality ratings to Participants, Network Providers, referring providers, or the public."

- **Participants and referring providers.** This is close to a word-for-word mirror of what § 724(a)(1)(A) forbids. The statute protects the plan's ability to give provider-specific *cost or quality* information "through ... any other means" to participants, beneficiaries, eligible individuals and referring providers. A contractual ban on telling participants which providers cost less or rate better is the paradigm gag clause.
- **Network Providers.** "Network Providers" overlaps with "referring providers": a network physician who refers a patient is a referring provider. The restriction is prohibited at least to that extent.
- **The public.** The ban on public disclosure is the one part of § 9.5 that § 724(a)(2) permits, if reasonable. It is still in tension with the Transparency in Coverage machine-readable file requirements.
- **The last sentence does not cure the problem.** It says Meridian will provide member cost estimates "to the extent required by applicable transparency laws". The statute protects the *plan's* freedom to share by "any other means", not just through Meridian's tool.

### B. Section 9.4: Third-party recipients and the "Competitor" definition (prohibited as applied to business associates; high confidence)

Section 9.4 bars the Plan from disclosing Claims Data or Standard Reports, or directing Meridian to disclose them, to "any third party, including any consultant, actuary, auditor, broker, or successor administrator", without Meridian's prior written consent. Consent may not be unreasonably withheld only if the recipient:
1. signs the TPDUA,
2. "is not a Competitor", and
3. limits use to a purpose "approved in writing by Meridian".

"Competitor" (§ 1.3) means anyone offering, directly or through an affiliate, TPA, network, PBM, **claims auditing, or employee benefits consulting** services anywhere in the United States.

- **The direct conflict.** Section 724(a)(1)(C) protects the plan's right to share (A)/(B) data, "or direct[] that such data be shared", with a HIPAA business associate. Consultants, actuaries, auditors and successor TPAs are the classic plan business associates. A clause that requires Meridian's consent, and lets Meridian define the permitted purpose, is a direct restriction on exactly that.
- **The Competitor definition makes it worse.** It sweeps in essentially every consultant and every claims auditor. For those recipients, consent is entirely discretionary, which is the scenario the Departments' FAQ example describes.
- **Claimsight is a Competitor too.** Claimsight offers "claims auditing", so it is a "Competitor" under the ASA's own definition. Meridian's "no objection in principle" to Claimsight is a matter of grace, not contract right.
- **Successor administrators.** By definition every successor administrator is a Competitor. Combined with §§ 9.8 and 12.5, this gives Meridian a veto over data flowing to its replacement for up to three years after termination.
- **What Meridian may do.** To the extent § 9.4 governs disclosures to non-business associates (for example, the general public or other vendors not acting for the Plan), it is more defensible. And a reasonable confidentiality and data-security undertaking from a business associate recipient (use only for the Plan, no disclosure to other clients, no public disclosure) is unlikely to be treated as a "restriction". What the statute does not tolerate is a Meridian veto over *who* the Plan's business associate is or *what purpose* the Plan may pursue with its own data.
- **One nuance Meridian may raise.** A "business associate" under 45 C.F.R. § 160.103 is someone who handles PHI on the covered entity's behalf. If Northgate received *only* de-identified data, Meridian could argue it is not technically a business associate. This is easily addressed. Northgate and Claimsight should be engaged under Plan BAAs to perform functions that genuinely involve PHI, such as the claims audit and consulting on PHI-based analyses. This is common practice for benefits consultants and auditors and is consistent with the Northgate letter.

### C. Section 9.3, with §§ 1.4 and 9.1: Access, decline right, fees and timing

**1. The decline right (prohibited; high confidence).**
- The mechanism: Section 9.3 lets Meridian "decline any request for a custom extract that Meridian reasonably determines would require disclosure of Meridian Proprietary Information". "Meridian Proprietary Information" (§ 1.4) expressly includes "Allowed Amounts" and "any compilation of Claims Data that would reveal" Meridian's rates. Section 9.1 then claims Meridian ownership of proprietary information "embedded in or derivable from Claims Data".
- The effect: Meridian has contractual discretion to refuse the very data elements § 724(a)(1)(B)(i) and (ii) name: "the allowed amount" and provider "name and clinical designation".
- Meridian's own conduct shows the risk is real: Mr. Delacroix wrote that data governance is reviewing whether it can decline Northgate's request on this basis. The "reasonably determines" qualifier does not help when the definition makes every allowed amount proprietary.
- This is the clearest Article 9 violation in our view, and it closely tracks the FAQ example of access at the TPA's discretion.

**2. The standard extract does not satisfy § 724(a)(1)(B).**
- Meridian's position is that the portal extract is "electronic access to de-identified claims data, which is what the CAA requires".
- The portal extract contains service category, provider type, geography, paid amounts and member cost share. It omits claim-level allowed amounts and provider identifiers (§ 9.2; Delacroix 9/18).
- The statute does not stop at "some" de-identified data. It specifies, "on a per claim basis", allowed amounts, provider name and clinical designation, service codes, and "any other data element included in claim or encounter transactions".
- A standard extract built specifically to leave out the enumerated elements does not discharge the obligation. Moving those elements behind a fee-and-discretion gate is a restriction.

**3. Fees and turnaround (likely prohibited as structured; moderate confidence; law unsettled).**
Mr. Delacroix is correct that § 724 does not expressly regulate format, frequency, turnaround or price. But the statute reaches agreements that restrict access "directly or indirectly". Several features together make the § 9.3 structure difficult to defend as a mere commercial term:
- **What the fee buys.** The fee attaches specifically to the data elements the statute enumerates. It is not a charge for genuinely bespoke analytics.
- **Size.** About $140k–$160k is roughly 7% of a year's administrative fees, for data Meridian already holds in claim transactions.
- **Unilateral pricing.** The price is set unilaterally at "then-current professional services rates".
- **No real deadline.** Delivery is 180 days after Meridian's "written acceptance", and there is no deadline for acceptance (Delacroix 9/21). In effect, access has no outside date at all.
- **"Upon request."** The statute's phrase implies responsive access, not an open-ended queue.

A court or agency could reasonably conclude that a modest, cost-based charge and a reasonable delivery period are permissible. We would not advise the Committee that every fee is unlawful. But a pricing and timing structure that, combined with the decline right, can delay or deny the statutory data indefinitely is in our view an indirect restriction. We would characterize this conclusion as moderate confidence.

**4. Section 12.4 provides no cost-recovery hook.** Meridian may argue that compliance changes are chargeable under § 12.4 ("change in applicable law enacted after the Effective Date"). The CAA was enacted December 27, 2020, three years before the January 1, 2024 Effective Date, so § 12.4 does not apply.

### D. Section 9.6: Claims audit (moderate confidence)

Section 9.6 limits audits to once per plan year, a sample of no more than 250 claims drawn using Meridian's methodology, an auditor that is not a "Competitor" (which excludes every claims auditor, including Claimsight), the TPDUA, and on-premises or view-only portal review.

- **Where it is a gag clause.** Section 724 does not create an audit right as such. But Meridian is using § 9.6 to limit the electronic claim-level data the Plan's auditor (a business associate) may receive: view-only review, 250 claims, and "claim-level pricing fields" only through a separate priced engagement. To that extent, § 9.6 operates as a restriction under § 724(a)(1)(B) and (C).
- **The practical path around it.** If the Plan obtains its full claim-level data under § 724 and directs it to Claimsight, Claimsight can perform the electronic, 100%-population portion of the audit *with the Plan's own data* without relying on § 9.6. Meridian may argue that any audit, however performed, is governed by § 9.6. We think that argument is weak as applied to analysis of data the Plan is statutorily entitled to receive and share.
- **Where it is not a gag clause, but still a problem.** Limits on access to Meridian's claim files, adjudication systems, and claims-editing logic are more likely to be treated as permissible commercial terms under § 724. They remain a significant **fiduciary prudence** concern. A 250-claim, TPA-selected, view-only audit of a $71 million claims book is a thin monitoring tool, and the Committee should negotiate a materially broader right (Part VII).

### E. Section 12.5 and § 9.8: Transition (moderate–high confidence)

On termination, Meridian provides transition data in its "then-standard extract format" at professional services rates, "subject to Sections 9.4 and 9.5". Those sections survive for three years (§ 9.8). Because every successor TPA is a "Competitor", Meridian can withhold consent to delivering claims data to the successor, which would be a business associate of the Plan. The standard-format limitation would also strip allowed amounts and provider identifiers. This restricts § 724(a)(1)(B)/(C) access exactly when the Plan most needs it. It also directly affects a 2028 transition.

### F. The Third-Party Data Use Agreement (moderate–high confidence)

The Plan does not sign the TPDUA, but §§ 9.4 and 9.6 make it a condition of any third-party access, so its terms operate as part of the ASA's restrictions:
- **¶ 1** forbids "any benchmarking or repricing analysis not expressly included in the Approved Purpose" approved by Meridian. That gives Meridian control over whether the Plan's consultant may benchmark Meridian.
- **¶ 2** requires the recipient to represent that it is not a "Competitor". **Neither Northgate nor Claimsight could truthfully sign this**, given the § 1.3 definition.
- **¶ 3** forbids disclosing the data "to Client except in aggregated form approved by Meridian". That restricts the flow of provider-specific cost information to the plan sponsor (§ 724(a)(1)(A)) and the plan's access through its business associate (§ 724(a)(1)(B)/(C)).

The security, destruction and audit provisions (¶¶ 4–6) are generally reasonable in concept.

### G. Section 9.7 does not cure the problem (moderate–high confidence)

Mr. Delacroix's position is that § 9.7 means there is "by definition" no gag clause. We disagree, for five reasons:

1. **The statute regulates agreements that "would directly or indirectly restrict."** A provision that Meridian actually invokes to refuse consent, gate data behind fees, and reserve a right to decline is a restriction in operation, whatever a boilerplate savings clause says. If a generic savings clause cured specific prohibited terms, the prohibition would be meaningless: every TPA could keep its restrictions and add one sentence.
2. **The first sentence covers only "access to information."** It does not mention *providing* information to participants or referring providers (§ 724(a)(1)(A); ASA § 9.5) or *sharing* with business associates (§ 724(a)(1)(C); ASA § 9.4). Those are the two highest-confidence violations, and § 9.7 does not even reach them on its terms.
3. **The second sentence works only after a court or agency rules.** It modifies a provision only once "determined by a court or governmental authority of competent jurisdiction to be prohibited". Until then the provision stays in force. The Plan would have to litigate or obtain an agency ruling to get its data, and that burden is itself a restriction.
4. **Meridian is not applying § 9.7 as a rule of construction.** If Meridian believed § 9.7 meant what its account executive says, it would deliver the data and consent to the Plan's business associates. Its conduct shows the specific provisions control in practice.
5. **The Departments have not, to our knowledge, said whether savings clauses cure gag clauses.** We therefore rate this conclusion moderate–high rather than high. We think the better reading of the statute is clear.

**Turning § 9.7 into leverage.** Section 9.7 is Meridian's own written promise that "nothing in this Article 9 shall be construed to restrict Plan Sponsor's access to information in a manner prohibited by" § 724. The Plan should hold Meridian to it. A demand for the statutory data, coupled with § 9.7, sets up a clean breach-of-contract position if Meridian refuses (see Part VII.C).

### H. Meridian's provider agreements

Meridian's 2024 email also represented that its provider network agreements contain no gag clauses. We have not seen them. The Plan is not a party to those contracts, but restrictions in them could flow through to the Plan's access. The amendment should include a representation and indemnity covering them (Appendix A).

### I. Overall conclusion on Q1

At least three provisions (§ 9.5 as to participants and referring providers, § 9.4 as applied to business associates, and the § 9.3 decline right read with § 1.4) are, in our view, prohibited gag clauses, and they have been in the ASA since execution. We have high confidence that the ASA contains at least one prohibited gag clause, and so has since January 1, 2024. That conclusion does not depend on the unsettled fee and timing questions.

## V. QUESTION 2: NORTHGATE'S DATA REQUEST AND THE CLAIMSIGHT AUDIT

### A. Can Meridian block Northgate as a "Competitor"?

**No, not lawfully as to the § 724 data, provided Northgate receives it as the Plan's business associate.** The § 1.3 Competitor carve-out in § 9.4 is, as applied to Plan business associates, a restriction the Plan was prohibited from agreeing to. Meridian's stated reason (its consulting affiliate competes with Northgate) is a commercial interest of Meridian's. It is not a legitimate basis for limiting the Plan's ability to evaluate Meridian.

**What Meridian can legitimately ask for:**
- reasonable confidentiality, use-limitation and data-security undertakings;
- a commitment that Northgate will not use Meridian's provider-level rate information for other clients or disclose it publicly;
- an internal firewall if Northgate has any affiliate that offers TPA or network services.

The Northgate letter already offers most of this.

**The genuinely difficult point** is Northgate's refusal to accept a purpose restriction that would block use "in a market check". We recommend this line:
- The Committee and Northgate may use the analysis for the market check and any decision to remain with or replace Meridian.
- Northgate will not give Meridian's claim-level allowed amounts or provider-level rate schedules to competing bidders.
- Repricing exercises with bidders should use billed charges, codes and utilization, not Meridian's allowed amounts.

This protects Meridian's legitimate interest without restricting the Plan.

**Meridian's offer of a free "network performance report"** from its own consulting affiliate is not a substitute. A fiduciary relying on the monitored vendor's affiliate to benchmark that vendor would have difficulty showing a prudent, independent process. The Committee should decline it as a substitute, though it may accept it as supplemental information.

### B. Structuring the request so it is legally clean

The request should be restructured before it goes back to Meridian:

1. **Formal request through counsel to Meridian's legal department, from the Plan (the Committee as Plan Administrator).** It should invoke:
   - § 724(a)(1)(B)–(C);
   - ASA § 9.7;
   - **BAA § 3.2**, which requires Meridian to make PHI available to the Plan "or to a person designated by Covered Entity" for health care operations, "including the evaluation of Business Associate's performance". Auditing and cost-management analyses are health care operations under 45 C.F.R. § 164.501;
   - BAA § 9.1 (the BAA controls over the ASA as to PHI);
   - Plan § 8.6 (the Committee may require the Claims Administrator to furnish records).
2. **Designate Northgate and Claimsight as Plan business associates receiving the data on the Plan's behalf.** Confirm both have signed Plan BAAs and that their SOWs describe the permitted uses. The Northgate SOW is dated August 20, 2026. We have not seen a Claimsight engagement.

   - *The problem.* Northgate's field list includes claim numbers and exact dates of service, receipt and payment. Under the HIPAA safe harbor (45 C.F.R. § 164.514(b)(2)), all date elements other than year that relate to an individual must be removed. Claim numbers may also need treatment. As drafted, the request is not "de-identified" under safe harbor, and Meridian will say so. (Provider NPIs and names are *not* patient identifiers and need not be removed. Under BAA § 9.1, the § 9.4 consent requirement should not override this.

4. **Keep PHI away from Halvorsen as employer.** The Committee and Halvorsen HR or finance should receive only de-identified or summary analyses. ## VI. QUESTION 3: THE GAG CLAUSE ATTESTATIONS

### A. The 2024 and 2025 attestations already filed

**Accuracy.** Both attestations stated without qualification that the Plan was "in compliance with the gag clause prohibition for the attestation period". The ASA, signed December 2023 and effective January 1, 2024, contained §§ 9.3–9.6 throughout both periods. Given our Part IV conclusions, both attestations were very likely inaccurate.

**Exposure, which we assess as low to moderate if addressed promptly:**

1. **No stand-alone penalty for an inaccurate attestation.** Section 724(a)(3) imposes the attestation duty, but ERISA has no specific civil penalty for an inaccurate GCPCA for a private-employer plan.
2. **Criminal false statement (18 U.S.C. § 1001).** This requires a *knowing and willful* false statement. The record shows the opposite: Ms. Raman relied on Meridian's express written confirmation and invitation to rely, and did not review the contract. We consider criminal exposure on the past filings remote. **That changes for the future.** The Plan and Ms. Raman now have notice, so a clean 2026 attestation would not have the same defense.
3. **The underlying gag clause violation.**
   - *DOL.* In an investigation, EBSA will typically request service provider agreements. An attestation that cannot be squared with the ASA invites scrutiny and corrective-action demands. The practical remedy is removing the provisions, which the Plan is now pursuing.
   - *IRS excise tax.* IRC § 9824 is enforced through the § 4980D excise tax: $100 per day per affected individual, subject to self-reporting on Form 8928. How § 4980D applies to a gag clause is genuinely **unclear**: who is an "individual to whom the failure relates", whether the failure is a one-time event (entering into the agreement) or continuing, and what "correction" means. We are not aware of IRS guidance on these points. Relevant mitigants include:
     - § 4980D(c)(1): no tax for periods when the failure was not discovered despite reasonable diligence;
     - § 4980D(c)(2): no tax if the failure is due to reasonable cause and is corrected within 30 days of discovery;
     - § 4980D(c)(3): for reasonable-cause failures in a single-employer plan, an annual cap of the lesser of 10% of the prior year's group health plan costs or $500,000;
     - § 4980D(c)(4): waiver authority.
     The 30-day correction rule is a further reason to act quickly once the Plan has knowledge. We recommend a focused review with Halvorsen's tax advisers of whether a Form 8928 filing is required for 2024–2026 and of the reasonable-cause position. We do not recommend filing or not filing until that review is done.
   - *Participant litigation.* Recent fiduciary suits against employers over health plan costs (e.g., *Lewandowski v. Johnson & Johnson* (D.N.J.) and *Navarro v. Wells Fargo & Co.* (D. Minn.)) have largely been dismissed on standing grounds. Plans have also sued TPAs over access to claims data (e.g., Kraft Heinz's 2023 suit against Aetna). *Cite-check before external use.* An inaccurate attestation is not itself a cause of action, but it would be unhelpful evidence in a monitoring claim. The Committee's current process (identifying the issue, getting advice, correcting) is the best defense.
4. **Claims against Meridian.** Meridian's November 6, 2024 email said the ASA does not contain gag clauses and the Plan "may rely on this confirmation". That supports a negligent misrepresentation claim and possibly contractual indemnity (Article 8, which we have not reviewed), covering costs, any excise tax and remediation expenses. Preserve the email and the September 2026 chain.

**Recommended handling of the prior attestations:**
- **Document the good-faith reliance record now:** the Meridian email, Ms. Raman's note, and the Committee's September 30 minutes.
- **Correct the record.** First, confirm with CMS (through the HIOS help desk or the GCPCA instructions) whether a prior-period attestation can be amended or resubmitted. If it can, consider corrective filings once the amendment status is known. In any event, use the 2026 attestation's Additional Information field to disclose that:
  - (i) in October 2026 the Plan's review identified provisions in its TPA agreement, in effect since January 1, 2024, that may constitute prohibited gag clauses;
  - (ii) the 2024 and 2025 attestations were submitted in good-faith reliance on the TPA's written confirmation; and
  - (iii) the steps taken to remediate.

  A separate voluntary approach to EBSA is a judgment call. We would not recommend it before the Meridian negotiation plays out, unless the HIOS correction route is unavailable and the Committee prefers to be proactive.
- **Do not change the prior filings quietly** or recharacterize them without counsel.
- **Notify insurers if required.** Review the fiduciary liability policy for notice-of-circumstances requirements. A late notice can forfeit coverage.
- **Check the 2023 Cordell filing date.** If Cordell filed after December 14, 2023, the Meridian ASA had already been signed when that attestation was submitted. This is a minor point, but it should be checked.

### B. The attestation due December 31, 2026

**The Plan must file.** Failing to file is a separate violation and would look worse than a qualified filing. The 2026 attestation period will likely run from about December 20, 2025 to the December 2026 submission date.

**Options:**
